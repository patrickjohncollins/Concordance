# The Story of Concordance

Patrick Collins

## The heart of the story

We became human by caring for each other, and we can stay human the same way.

For almost all of our history, people survived because they shared: food around a fire, children raised by many hands, help when the harvest failed. We built astonishing things on that foundation: cities, science, a network that reaches nearly every person alive. Yet today more of us live alone, more of what we need is bought rather than given, and the earth that feeds us is under strain. The care that made us is still everywhere, in a pantry on a street corner, a work day in a valley, a neighbor's knock on a hot afternoon. But it is scattered and fragile.

The story of Concordance is the story of gathering it again: not by going back, and not by waiting for someone in charge, but by people in small circles choosing to look after each other, joining with other circles, and passing it on. It is told through many lives, in many places and times, each one small, together making a whole.

## The hearth

One image carries the whole story, and every culture knows it: **the fire people gather around.**

The first hearth was humanity's first commons. Around it, people shared food, kept each other warm, told stories, decided together and watched over the sleeping. It was a circle before there were rooms, and its light reached exactly as far as a group of people who knew each other's names.

The story follows the hearth through time:

**Then:** a fire in the open, a circle of faces, food passed hand to hand.

**Later:** the hearth moves indoors, into each house, and each household warms itself alone.

**Now:** the glow in most homes is a screen, connecting us to everyone and, too often, to no one in particular.

**What could be:** a small device in the corner of a home, also called a hearth, that keeps a circle's memory safe and links it to circles everywhere, while the real fire is lit again at shared tables, gardens and work days.

Four other images recur beside it:

**The circle:** people facing each other, no head of the table.

**The knock at the door:** the moment someone comes to see if you're all right.

**The seed:** something small, given away, that grows where it lands: a sapling, then a tree, then a forest whose roots are joined underground, so the strong trees feed the struggling ones, and every tree drops seeds of its own.

**The string that sounds:** pluck one string, and another tuned to the same note begins to sing, though no one touched it. This is how Concordance finds its people: it does not push, it sounds, and those already tuned to it answer. The name says it too: in music, a concord is notes that sound well together.

## Three movements

The story moves through three times, honestly, without romance or despair.

### How things were

We are a species that raises its young together; no human child survives alone. For most of our history we lived in small groups where everyone was known, food was shared, and help was expected. Across the world, people built lasting ways of caring for what they held in common: irrigation shared by turns, forests and pastures governed by village rules, work days where neighbors built each other's houses and ended with a meal. They had many names: minga, mutirão, dugnad, gotong royong, bayanihan, harambee.

It was never paradise. There was violence, hunger and hierarchy, and people tried many arrangements, sometimes choosing equality and sometimes losing it. But the knowledge of how to share, decide together and look after the vulnerable was carried everywhere, in every language.

### How things are

We live in the most connected and materially abundant time in history. Many diseases are rare, information is nearly free, and a message crosses the world in a second. Since 1990, about one and a half billion people have risen out of extreme poverty.

And yet. More people live alone than ever before, and loneliness has become a public health concern. Much of what was once given, such as care for children and elders, company, help with a move, is now bought, or goes undone. Wealth gathers in fewer hands. The climate is warming, forests and species are disappearing, and the poorest feel it first. The networks that connect us are mostly owned by a few companies, built to hold our attention rather than to help us care for each other.

Yet the old knowledge never left. Every month, by the United Nations' estimate, around two billion people volunteer, most of them simply helping a neighbor. When a pandemic came to the Philippines, one food pantry on a single street became hundreds within a week, and thousands within months. In a Brazilian valley, farmers still gather for mutirões. Care is everywhere. It just lacks a way to hold together.

### How things could be

Not a utopia, and not a return to the past, but something practical: small circles of people who know each other's names, sharing what they have, deciding together, putting need first, and free to come and go. Circles linked to other circles as equals, so help can travel to wherever a flood or a fire or a hard winter strikes. Land and shared things held in trust, so each generation inherits more than the last. Tools that belong to the people who use them, carrying memory and connection without anyone owning or watching it.

A world where no one goes unnoticed, conflicts end in repair, and the earth is tended for those not yet born. Built one circle at a time, and renewed by every generation that chooses it.

In time, perhaps, a people: one that shares a charter, a language and a story, and that anyone may join without leaving their own country, faith or culture behind. A nation built on consent and care rather than borders and force would be something new in the world.

## Many lives, one whole

A concordance is an old kind of book: an index showing every place a word appears across a great text, so a reader can see how one word lives in many passages. The story of Concordance can be built the same way.

**Each chapter is a word,** one of the simple acts the Charter asks of us:

*Gather. Answer. Notice. Share. Give. Work. Eat. Decide. Mend. Forgive. Welcome. Rest. Celebrate. Remember. Pass on. Hold in trust.*

**Within each chapter, the word appears in many lives,** across the three times:

someone long ago, for whom the word meant survival;

someone today, somewhere in the world, living it or missing it;

someone in a circle, years from now, for whom it has become ordinary.

Read one story, and it is a small human moment. Read a chapter, and the same act shines across thousands of years and every continent. Read the whole book, and the pattern appears: the same few acts of care, repeated in every age and culture, are what hold humanity together. That pattern is the argument of the book, shown rather than stated.

The threads also cross between chapters. The person who is helped in *Answer* gives back in *Work*; the one who causes harm in *Mend* is forgiven in *Forgive*; the child welcomed in *Welcome* passes it on in the final chapter. By the end, the many small lives are visibly one story.

## The threads

The story is carried by many people, some imagined, some real and told with their consent, inspired by accounts from every continent. A first set of threads:

**Gather**  
A grandmother who keeps the night fire for a band of foragers  
*Deep past*

**Share**  
Farmers taking turns with water from a shared channel  
*An old irrigation commons, centuries ago*

**Work**  
Neighbors carrying a family's house to new ground  
*A village, generations ago*

**Answer**  
Joseph, the retired bus driver who doesn't trust phones  
*A city in a heatwave, today*

**Give**  
A young woman who sets out a table of rice and vegetables with a sign: take what you need, give what you can  
*A street during a pandemic, today*

**Rest, and Remember**  
Women in a small savings circle who stand by a member after her husband dies  
*West Africa, today*

**Decide**  
A volunteer who brings refugees and the host village to the same table, though the first meetings are hard  
*A border region, today*

**Mend, and Forgive**  
Two neighbors who wronged each other, and the slow path back  
*A suburb, near future*

**Welcome**  
A translator carrying the Charter into her grandmother's language  
*Many places, near future*

**Notice**  
A circle that keeps every vulnerable member safe through a flood  
*A river valley, near future*

**Hold in trust**  
A child born on land held in trust, who plants trees she will never sit under  
*A generation from now*

**Pass on**  
An old woman telling the child how it all began, beside a hearth  
*The far future, at the end of the book*

**Guiding principles for the threads**

Every continent, faith and kind of place: villages and cities, rich and poor, deserts and coasts.

Real stories are told only with consent, in the people's own words where possible, and credited.

Hard truths stay in: loss, conflict, failure, and circles that fall apart.

No heroes and no villains: ordinary people, each with something to give and something to learn.

## Speaking to everyone, timelessly

For the story to reach people in every culture, and still ring true in a hundred years, it rests on what all people share rather than on what divides them.

**Universal moments, not current events.** Birth, hunger, illness, a storm, a death, a stranger at the door, a child learning a skill. Headlines date quickly; these never do.

**Images every culture holds.** Fire, bread, a door, hands, a river, a seed, a tree, a string that sounds, a name spoken aloud.

**Plain words that translate well.** Short sentences, concrete things, few abstractions, no jargon. The technology appears only as what it does for people.

**No single tradition's voice.** Each tradition appears in its own words (minga, dugnad, harambee, langar, sangha) as one voice among many, so every reader finds their own heritage inside the story.

**Honest about darkness.** The story names what we are doing to the earth and to each other, without blame of any group, and without despair.

**Hope as practice, not prophecy.** It never promises the world will be saved. It shows what people can do tomorrow morning, and lets the reader see that millions already are.

**The reader as the next thread.** Every chapter leaves a door open: this word is something you could do, today, where you live.

## An opening passage

A first draft of how the book might begin, in the chapter *Gather*:

> The night is cold, and the old woman is the last one awake.
>
> She feeds the fire one stick at a time, the way her mother showed her. Around it, the others sleep in a ring, close enough to touch: her daughter, her daughter's children, the young man who arrived from the river people in the spring, the hunter with the bad leg who cannot keep up anymore and whom no one has ever thought of leaving behind. Beyond the light, the dark is full of things that could kill them. Inside it, they are safe, because each of them is watched over by someone else.
>
> She does not have a word for what they are. If she did, it would mean something like *those who share this fire*.
>
> Fifty thousand years later, in a city she could never have imagined, a woman sets a small table on a street corner. On it she puts rice, cans of sardines, a few bunches of greens, and a hand-lettered sign: *Take what you need. Give what you can.* By evening the table is empty, and full again. Within a month there are hundreds like it, in streets she will never see.
>
> And somewhere ahead of us, on a winter night, a girl sits up late with her grandmother beside a stove, while in the corner a small box glows, quietly keeping the memory of their circle. "Tell me again," the girl says, "how it started."
>
> "It started," says her grandmother, "the way it always starts. Someone kept a fire going, and someone else came in from the cold."

## How it ends

The book ends where it began, at a hearth, with the last chapter, *Pass on*.

The grandmother from the opening finishes her story, and the reader realizes that every thread in the book has been part of it: the forager by the fire, the farmers sharing water, the woman with the table on the street corner, Joseph in the heat, the neighbors who forgave each other, the translator, the child planting trees. One long chain of people, each keeping the fire going for the next.

The book's shape follows the seed. The early chapters are seeds: single acts, single people, a cart on a street corner. The middle chapters are saplings: first circles, fragile, sometimes failing, needing shelter. The later chapters are trees: circles that have lasted, holding land in trust, shading people not yet born. And the ending is a forest, all the threads joined at the roots, each one feeding the others, each one dropping seeds.

Through it all runs a sound. In each life there is a moment of recognition, when a person meets Concordance and something in them answers, like a string that begins to sing when its note is played nearby. The reader is meant to feel that moment too, and to notice they were already tuned to it.

Then the story turns to the reader. Not with a demand or a program, but with the same simple invitation the Charter makes: find a few people, offer one thing you can give, and look out for each other.

The final page is left nearly blank, with room for the reader's own word, and their own story: the next thread.

## Notes and sources

The facts behind "How things are," checked on 22 November 2026:

- **Out of extreme poverty:** extreme poverty fell from about 2.3 billion people in 1990 to about 831 million in 2025. [World Bank, Poverty overview](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/poverty/overview)
- **More people live alone than ever before:** one-person households were under 10% of households for centuries; by 2018 they were about 28% worldwide, projected to reach 35% by mid-century. [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/one-person-households); [IPS, using UN data](https://www.ipsnews.net/2025/04/growth-one-person-households/)
- **Loneliness as a public health concern:** about 1 in 6 people worldwide are lonely, linked to more than 871,000 deaths a year. [WHO Commission on Social Connection, 2025](https://www.who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death)
- **Wealth gathers in fewer hands:** the richest 10% own about 75% of global wealth and the poorest half about 2%; the top 0.001%'s share rose from about 4% in 1995 to over 6%. World Inequality Report 2026, as reported by [bdnews24](https://bdnews24.com/economy/e05a9f73ebe2)
- **The climate is warming:** 2015–2025 were the eleven warmest years on record; 2025 was about 1.43 °C above the pre-industrial average. [WMO, State of the Global Climate 2025](https://wmo.int/node/38416)
- **Species disappearing:** around 1 million animal and plant species are threatened with extinction. [IPBES Global Assessment, 2019](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/press-release/natures-dangerous-decline-unprecedented-species-extinction-rates)
- **The poorest feel it first:** the richest 10% produce close to half of emissions but bear about 3% of relative income losses. World Inequality Lab, via [Inequality.org](https://inequality.org/facts/global-inequality/)
- **Two billion volunteers:** about 2.1 billion people, 34.5% of the working-age population, volunteer each month, mostly directly for other people rather than through organizations. [UNV, State of the World's Volunteerism Report 2026](https://knowledge.unv.org/evidence-library/state-of-the-worlds-volunteerism-report-2026); [ILO](https://ilostat.ilo.org/blog/new-global-estimates-reveal-that-2-1-billion-people-volunteer-monthly/)
- **The Philippine pantries:** Ana Patricia Non set up a bamboo cart on Maginhawa Street, Quezon City, on 14 April 2021; more than a hundred pantries existed by 20 April, and about 6,700 within months. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_pantries_in_the_Philippines); [Tatler Asia](https://www.tatlerasia.com/power-purpose/philanthropy/ana-patricia-non-community-pantry); [Global Voices](https://globalvoices.org/2021/05/05/community-pantries-inspire-hope-amid-pandemic-and-economic-crisis-in-the-philippines/)
- **Mutirões:** collective work days among Brazilian farmers continue today, including agroforestry mutirões. [Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/mutirao); [Federal University of Viçosa, 2024](https://locus.ufv.br/bitstreams/53aa9e8c-ced0-419d-b995-bdf67b128d1d/download)

Not sourced, because they are interpretation rather than fact: that much once-given care is now bought, and that the main networks are owned by a few companies and built for attention. If the book states either as fact, it will need a source.

# Who Knocks

Joseph Mensah did not trust anything that lived in a phone. He had driven the number 14 bus for thirty-one years, and in his experience, the things people said would make life easier mostly made it more complicated.

So when his granddaughter set up the circle app on his old handset, he let her, the way you let a child show you a drawing.

"You have to put one thing you can offer," Efua said.

"I don't need anything."

"Not need. Offer."

He thought about it. "I know every street in this city," he said finally. "Every shortcut. Every road that floods."

She typed it in. She also ticked a box he didn't read, the one that said *Check on me if it gets dangerous*. Then she went back to her own city, four hours away, and Joseph forgot about it.

For eight months, nothing happened. Once, someone asked him the quickest way to the eye hospital, and he told them, and felt briefly important. Mostly he sat on his balcony and complained about the girl downstairs, who rode her scooter too fast and played music at night.

Then came the heat.

It arrived in early April and didn't leave. By the second week, the power failed every afternoon. By the third, it failed for whole days. Joseph's fan stopped. His fridge stopped. He lay on his bed with a wet cloth on his face and told himself he had survived worse.

On the fourth day without power, someone knocked.

It was the scooter girl. She was carrying two bottles of water and a sheet of paper.

"Mr Mensah? You're on the list." She held it up: his name, his floor, the box Efua had ticked. "There's a cool room at the school. They have solar. Can you walk?"

He wanted to say no. He wanted to say he didn't need anything. But his legs were shaking, so he let her take his arm.

The cool room was a classroom full of old people, babies and their mothers, all lying on mats under fans that actually turned. Someone handed him a cup of tea. Someone else asked if he had his medicines.

He was there for an hour before he heard the problem. The buses had stopped. Three people in the room needed dialysis at the hospital across the river, and the main bridge road was flooded from a burst pipe. The volunteers with cars didn't know any other way.

Joseph sat up.

"Take the old cattle road behind the brewery," he said. "Then cut through the market on Third Street. It's narrow, but it's dry. I drove it every morning when they rebuilt the bridge in '09."

The young man with the car keys stared at him. "Can you show me?"

So Joseph Mensah, who did not trust anything that lived in a phone, rode in the front seat for six trips across the city that night, directing drivers down roads nobody else remembered. Somewhere around the fourth trip, he realised he was laughing.

The heat broke two weeks later. The power came back. Things went back to almost normal.

Except that now, on Thursday evenings, Joseph sits in the community hall with a paper map of the city spread across two tables, teaching a group of young drivers every shortcut he knows. The scooter girl is one of them. She still rides too fast, but she slows down outside his building.

And on his phone, under *Things I can offer*, there's now a second line, which he typed himself, slowly, with one finger:

*I can check on people. I know what it's like to be alone in the heat.*
